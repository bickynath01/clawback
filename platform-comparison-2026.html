What a Return Really Costs a Reseller: eBay vs Poshmark vs Mercari vs Depop vs Whatnot vs Vinted (2026)

LAST UPDATED: OCTOBER 2026

A $50 sale does not have one universal return cost. The same $50 item can come back to you on two different marketplaces and create two very different losses.

On one platform, your selling fee may come back and the return label may be prepaid. On another, a fixed transaction fee can remain behind. On another, the biggest problem may not be the platform fee at all — it may be that the item comes back worth $20 instead of the $50 you could have sold it for.

That is why comparing marketplaces only by their advertised selling fee can be misleading. For a reseller, the more useful question is:

"If this sale goes wrong, how much of my money am I actually exposed to losing?"

This guide compares eBay, Poshmark, Mercari, Depop, Whatnot and Vinted from that seller-loss perspective. The rules below are based on current platform policies and help pages checked in October 2026. Marketplace policies can change, so use the official policy links provided below when a specific dispute is on the line.

The quick answer: the lowest selling fee isn't automatically the lowest return risk

If you only look at the fee charged when an item sells, Vinted looks very different from Poshmark. If you only look at return shipping, Mercari's weight threshold matters. If you only look at payment processing, Whatnot's fee structure needs more attention than its headline commission. And if you are worried about an item coming back damaged or worth less, the percentage taken at the original sale is only part of the story.

A simple $50 example makes the differences easier to see. Assume: $50 item price, no buyer-paid shipping, no sales tax, no promoted listing or boost, no other optional charges.

For Whatnot, the example uses an 8% standard U.S. commission rate, which applies to many categories but is not universal. Whatnot's current system varies by category and four-week sales tier.

That is useful, but it still doesn't answer the question a seller usually cares about after a return: What happens to those fees when the buyer gets their money back?

The real comparison starts when the buyer wants a refund

A refund doesn't automatically mean that every dollar connected to the original sale disappears. Different platforms treat fees, shipping and returned merchandise differently. This is where a $50 sale can become a $50 problem — or a much smaller one.

eBay: the return can create three separate loss points

eBay is the platform where sellers need to separate the loss into pieces. For most categories, eBay's basic fee structure is 13.6% of the total amount of the sale up to $7,500, plus a $0.30 per-order fee for orders of $10 or less or $0.40 for orders over $10. The total amount of the sale can include the item price, buyer-paid shipping, sales tax and certain other amounts.

But the return reason matters. For eligible buyer-requested full refunds — such as changed mind, ordered by mistake, doesn't fit or found a better price — eBay's fee-credit policy can allow eligible final value and per-order fee credits. For certain seller-responsibility returns, such as an item that arrives damaged or is not as described, the variable final value fee may be credited while the fixed per-order fee is not. That difference is easy to miss if you only look at the headline 13.6%.

Example

  • Sale: $60
  • eBay final value fee: 13.6% of the applicable sale amount
  • Per-order fee: $0.40
  • INAD return
  • Seller pays an $8.50 return label
  • Returned item can now realistically be sold for $25

The seller's exposure isn't simply "eBay refunded the buyer." You have to look at: return shipping + unrecovered item value + any fee that wasn't credited. That's the important distinction.

Poshmark: the fee is simple; the item can be the expensive part

Poshmark's standard U.S. seller fee is straightforward: $2.95 for sales under $15, and 20% for sales of $15 or more. Poshmark also provides prepaid return labels for approved Posh Protect returns. That means a seller shouldn't automatically assume that a $50 approved return creates a $10 Poshmark commission loss.

The bigger question can become: What condition is the item in when it comes back? That's particularly important for clothing, shoes and other condition-sensitive inventory. A clean $40 item that comes back worth $15 has a $25 value loss, even if the original commission is reversed.

A real seller problem

This isn't just a theoretical scenario. Seller discussions regularly describe returns arriving with stains, missing tags, snags or other condition changes. One recent Poshmark seller described receiving a returned jacket with snags, smells and detached tags after sending it out new with tags, and described the financial impact as substantial. Another seller discussion described a return arriving with large holes cut into the item. These are individual reports, not evidence that Poshmark generally mishandles returns — but they show why returned-item condition is a recurring seller concern.

That is why the Poshmark question isn't simply "How much does Poshmark charge?" It's also: "What is this item worth if it comes back in a different condition?"

Mercari: the fee is predictable, but heavy returns change the equation

Mercari currently charges sellers a 10% selling fee on the item price plus buyer-paid shipping for the applicable listings. There is no separate seller payment-processing fee under the current structure. For approved returns, Mercari provides a prepaid return label for items up to 50 pounds. That 50-pound threshold is where a heavier item becomes a different seller problem.

For returns above 50 pounds, the seller must provide the return shipping label. Mercari says the seller has three business days to provide it; otherwise the transaction can be canceled.

So the useful question for a Mercari seller isn't only "What is Mercari's 10% fee?" For bulky inventory, ask: "What happens if this thing comes back?" A 2-pound jacket and a 60-pound piece of equipment are not the same return-risk calculation.

And the seller community shows why condition and identity matter too. Sellers have described situations where returned merchandise allegedly came back damaged or different from what was originally shipped. Those individual experiences don't establish a general Mercari outcome, but they reinforce the value of keeping clear pre-shipment evidence.

Depop: low seller fees change what the loss looks like

For U.S. sellers using Depop Payments, Depop currently charges: no Depop selling fee, and 3.3% + $0.45 payment processing. The processing fee is calculated on the item sale price plus shipping and applicable taxes. That creates a very different return picture from a platform where a large percentage commission remains part of the original cost.

When a Depop Payments sale is refunded, Depop says the applicable fees are reversed automatically. So if a $30 item comes back damaged and its realistic resale value falls to $10, the main financial problem isn't a permanently retained selling commission. It's the $20 drop in the item's value, plus any seller-paid costs that apply to the specific situation.

Depop's return policy also distinguishes genuine item problems from ordinary buyer's remorse. Depop Protection covers situations such as severe damage, wrong items, incorrect sizes, counterfeit items and major undisclosed flaws, while simply deciding that an item doesn't fit or doesn't look right isn't automatically covered. That distinction matters because "return" is not one single scenario.

Whatnot: don't confuse the commission rate with the total cost

Whatnot is the platform where the current fee structure deserves a warning label. As of September 21, 2026, Whatnot uses a four-week sales-tier system. Your total sales during the period determine your tier for the next period, and the commission rate varies by category. In the U.S., the standard tier starts at 8% for Sports and TCG, 8% for Fashion, 8% for many other categories, and lower rates in some categories such as Coins. Higher sales tiers can reduce the commission rate.

Payment processing is separate: 2.9% + $0.30 per transaction in the U.S. And Whatnot says processing is calculated on the total order value paid by the buyer, including the item price, buyer-paid shipping and taxes. So an advertised commission percentage isn't the same thing as your total effective fee.

A second important difference

Whatnot's current seller-support system makes refund responsibility more explicit than older comparisons suggested. For certain problems — such as an item not matching the description, an incorrect item, defective merchandise or damage in transit — sellers may be responsible for resolving the request. If the seller provides a refund after a returned item comes back, Whatnot says the seller's ledger can be adjusted for the payout, buyer-paid shipping, payment processing and return shipping, depending on the situation. If Whatnot Support determines the seller is responsible after an escalation, the seller may also be charged for associated refund costs and commission.

That means Whatnot's return exposure isn't well represented by "8% commission." It depends on the category, tier, order value, support outcome and refund scenario. And for a returned shipment, Whatnot currently tells sellers to contact Support within seven days of receiving the returned package so the situation can be reviewed.

Vinted: the $0 seller-fee advantage is real — but returns still matter

Vinted is the easiest platform to misunderstand if you stop at the seller-fee number. Vinted currently advertises zero selling fees, and says sellers keep 100% of what they earn. Buyers pay shipping. That makes Vinted the natural baseline in a fee comparison. But zero selling fees does not mean zero return risk.

Under Vinted's current U.S. refund policy:

  • buyers generally have 2 calendar days to report an issue after delivery;
  • qualifying issues include damaged orders and items significantly not as described;
  • buyers generally pay return shipping;
  • sellers can choose to cover return shipping by providing a Vinted-generated label;
  • items returned for a refund must meet Vinted's return conditions.

Vinted also says an item that simply isn't wanted, doesn't fit or doesn't suit the buyer isn't automatically eligible for a return when it matches the description. That's an important seller distinction.

Why sellers can still be surprised

A recent Vinted US discussion involved a buyer who was surprised to learn that the buyer, rather than the seller, could be responsible for return postage even when the dispute involved an item arriving damaged. Again, that is an individual user's experience, not a substitute for Vinted's policy — but it illustrates the exact kind of platform-rule confusion that causes sellers and buyers to make incorrect assumptions.

$0 seller fees does not mean every return is cost-free. It means the platform isn't taking a normal seller transaction fee from the sale.

The $50 sale isn't the best test. The $50 → $20 return is.

This is the comparison most fee tables miss. Suppose the exact same item sells for $50 on every platform. Later, it comes back and you realistically believe you can only sell it for $20. You have already lost: $50 − $20 = $30 of item value. Now ask what each platform adds on top.

PlatformMain question after a $50 → $20 return
eBayWas the return seller-responsibility? Was the fixed fee credited? Who paid return shipping? Can any seller protection apply?
PoshmarkWas the return covered by Posh Protect or another return program? What condition is the item in now?
MercariWas the return within the prepaid-label weight limit? What condition did the item return in?

This is why there isn't one honest answer to "Which marketplace has the cheapest returns?" The answer changes depending on why the item was returned, how much it weighs, what it is worth afterward, and who is responsible for the refund.

Who pays return shipping on each platform?

This is one of the most useful questions to ask before choosing where to list an item.

eBay

For returns involving an item that is damaged, faulty, not as described or the wrong item, the seller is generally responsible for the return shipping. Buyer-remorse returns can follow different rules depending on the seller's return policy.

Poshmark

Approved Posh Protect returns use Poshmark's return process and prepaid return label.

Mercari

Approved returns up to 50 pounds use a Mercari-provided prepaid label. For returns over 50 pounds, the seller must provide the label.

Depop

For qualifying U.S. returns, buyers receive a prepaid return label. Depop's policy says items must be returned within seven days of the return being agreed.

Whatnot

When a seller requires an item to be returned as part of a refund, the seller can be responsible for return shipping. Whatnot's current seller-support guidance explains how the associated ledger adjustment works.

Vinted

The buyer generally pays return shipping under Vinted's current refund policy, although the seller can choose to cover it with a Vinted-generated label.

That one difference alone can make the platforms feel very different to sellers.

Do sellers get their fees back when a buyer returns an item?

Again, there isn't one six-platform rule.

eBay

Sometimes. Eligible fee credits depend on the reason for the return and how the refund was handled. Some buyer-requested full refunds can qualify for both eligible final value and per-order fee credits. Certain seller-responsibility returns do not credit the fixed per-order fee.

Poshmark

The standard commission is generally reversed on approved returns.

Mercari

The seller's 10% selling fee is part of the transaction economics, but the exact treatment depends on the return/refund process and applicable policy.

Depop

Depop says applicable fees are reversed when a Depop Payments sale is refunded.

Whatnot

Refund costs can be charged back to the seller depending on who is determined responsible and how the refund is handled.

Vinted

There is no standard seller selling fee to reverse.

That is why "20% vs 10% vs 0%" doesn't tell the whole story.

The seller problem most fee comparisons miss: the item itself

A return is not only a money movement. It is also an inventory event.

Before the sale: item value = $50→Ordinary successful return: potentially still $50→Damaged or altered return: potentially $35, $20, $10 — or effectively $0

That difference can be larger than the original platform fee. For clothing, shoes, electronics, collectibles and condition-sensitive goods, this is especially important. A $50 sale with a $5 platform fee can be a much better outcome than a $50 sale with a $2 platform fee if the first platform leaves you with a sellable item and the second scenario leaves you with an item you can no longer confidently resell.

That is why resale value after return deserves a place beside seller fees when you compare marketplaces.

What sellers keep worrying about: "What if the item comes back different?"

Across reseller discussions, the same concerns keep appearing in different forms:

  • the item comes back stained;
  • tags are missing;
  • the buyer used the item before returning it;
  • the packaging is damaged;
  • accessories are missing;
  • the seller believes a different item was returned;
  • the seller isn't sure which evidence will matter;
  • the refund happens before the seller understands what happened to the merchandise.

These are not reasons to assume every buyer is dishonest. They are reasons to build a process that does not depend on memory. Before shipping an expensive or condition-sensitive item, keep:

  1. clear listing photographs;
  2. close-ups of existing flaws;
  3. serial numbers or identifying marks where applicable;
  4. photographs of included accessories;
  5. photographs of the item immediately before packing;
  6. tracking information;
  7. important buyer messages.

When a return arrives, document its condition before cleaning, repairing, relisting or altering anything. That habit isn't glamorous. It is simply cheaper than trying to recreate the evidence after a dispute begins.

eBay vs Poshmark vs Mercari: where the seller-loss calculation changes most

These three platforms illustrate three different kinds of risk.

eBay

The fee system is more complicated, but the bigger issue is that a seller-responsibility return can combine: return shipping + unrecovered value + a non-credited fixed fee. That makes the exact return reason important.

Poshmark

The standard seller fee is high at 20% for $15+ sales, but approved returns don't necessarily leave that commission as the seller's permanent loss. The item itself can become the bigger concern.

Mercari

The 10% seller fee is simple. But if you sell large or heavy inventory, the 50-pound return threshold deserves attention before you list.

This is a good example of why the "cheapest selling fee" isn't necessarily the most useful way to choose a platform.

Depop vs eBay: why the same return can feel very different

Consider a $50 sale. On eBay, a typical most-category illustration starts with: 13.6% + $0.40 = $7.20. On U.S. Depop Payments, the same $50 item produces: 3.3% + $0.45 = $2.10. But the difference becomes even more interesting after a refund. Depop says applicable fees are reversed when the sale is refunded. eBay's fee-credit rules depend on the return reason.

So a seller comparing these two platforms should not ask only "Which one takes less from my sale?" Ask: "Which one leaves me exposed if the sale has to be refunded?" That is a much more useful comparison.

Poshmark vs Vinted: high seller fee versus zero seller fee

The contrast is even more obvious here. On a $50 item: Poshmark: $10 standard seller fee. Vinted: $0 selling fee. But the comparison shouldn't end there. Poshmark gives sellers a structured return system and prepaid return process for approved Posh Protect claims. Vinted has no standard seller selling fee, but its current refund policy generally places return shipping on the buyer unless the seller chooses to cover it.

So the two platforms shift different parts of the return experience. Low fee and low return exposure are not always the same thing.

Whatnot vs eBay: headline percentage versus actual transaction economics

Whatnot's current U.S. system is especially important to check before using old comparison tables. Whatnot's commission is now determined by: sales volume + four-week tier + category, and payment processing is separate. eBay's final value fee, meanwhile, is calculated on the applicable total amount of the sale, with category-specific rates and a per-order fee in the basic structure.

Neither platform should be summarized honestly as "Platform A charges 8%; Platform B charges 13.6%." The fee bases are different. And the refund mechanics are different too. For a seller deciding between the two, the useful comparison is: original fee + shipping exposure + refund exposure + item-value exposure.

So which platform is best for sellers worried about returns?

There isn't one universal winner. Instead, think about the type of inventory you sell.

If you sell lightweight clothing

You may care more about: percentage fees; condition changes; buyer-remorse returns; whether the item comes back sellable.

If you sell heavy items

Shipping rules can matter much more. Mercari's 50-pound return-label threshold is a good example.

If you sell high-value collectibles

Evidence, item identity, authenticity and condition become much more important.

If you sell on live-commerce platforms

Whatnot's current tiered commission system and refund/billback rules deserve attention.

If your biggest priority is minimizing seller fees

Vinted's zero selling fee and Depop's current U.S. no-selling-fee structure stand out — but that doesn't make every return scenario cost-free.

The number that matters most: your actual loss

A marketplace can advertise "Low fees." That doesn't tell you what happened to your money after a forced return. For a specific transaction, the useful calculation is:

Original sale value−fees you actually lose−return shipping you actually pay−value lost from the returned item−other unrecovered costs+money recovered or fee credits=ACTUAL SELLER LOSS

That's the number worth comparing. And that's why CLAWBACK doesn't stop at the platform's advertised commission.

FAQ: Platform return fees and seller loss

Which selling platform has the lowest seller fees?

For standard U.S. selling, Vinted currently has no selling fees, while Depop has no Depop selling fee for U.S. sellers but does charge payment processing. Mercari, Poshmark, eBay and Whatnot have their own seller-fee structures. The lowest headline fee is not necessarily the lowest total cost after a return.

Which platform charges sellers for returns?

It depends on the return reason and platform. eBay can place return-shipping responsibility on the seller for item-problem returns. Mercari can require sellers to provide the return label for returns over 50 pounds. Whatnot can charge sellers for return/refund costs when they are responsible. Vinted generally places return shipping on the buyer, although sellers can choose to cover it.

Do sellers get their fees back when a buyer returns an item?

Sometimes. Depop says applicable fees are reversed when a Depop Payments sale is refunded. Poshmark generally reverses its seller commission on approved returns. eBay's fee-credit rules depend heavily on the return reason and refund process.

What happens if a buyer returns an item damaged?

Document the item's condition immediately and compare it with your original evidence. The platform's seller-protection rules determine what can happen next. Don't assume the seller automatically receives the full original value back, and don't assume the seller automatically loses everything either.

Is Vinted really free for sellers?

Vinted currently says there are no selling fees and sellers keep 100% of what they earn. Buyers pay shipping. That does not mean every return has zero financial impact.

Is Whatnot still 8%?

Not as a universal rule. Whatnot introduced a four-week tiered commission structure on September 21, 2026. Rates vary by category and sales tier, and payment processing is separate.

Is Mercari still 10%?

Yes. Mercari's current U.S. seller fee is 10% of the item price plus buyer-paid shipping for applicable listings.

Which platform is safest if I am worried about damaged returns?

There isn't a single platform that eliminates the risk. The better question is which platform's return rules match the type of inventory you sell — and whether you have enough evidence to document the item's condition before it leaves you.
