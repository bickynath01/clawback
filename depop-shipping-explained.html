Depop Shipping Explained: Labels, Costs, Free Shipping & Who Really Pays

Welcome to The Shipping Desk. This is the column where I take the exact shipping questions real Depop sellers ask — the ones typed into Google at 1am while a sold item sits unboxed on the kitchen table — and answer them the way a friend who has already lost money on shipping would. No corporate fluff. Just the mechanics, the math, and the mistakes I made so you don't have to.

Shipping on Depop looks simple until it isn't. The buyer pays, right? But then Depop takes 10% of that shipping too. And someone told you "free shipping gets you more sales," but nobody told you what that does to a $14 tee. So let's sit down at the desk and work through every letter in the pile.

How this column works: each section is a real question from a seller. I give you the one-sentence answer first (so you can grab it and go), then the full explanation, the math, and the fix. If a question links to a deeper guide, follow it — that's where the receipts live.

First, the mechanics: how Depop shipping actually works

The short version: the buyer usually pays shipping at checkout; Depop either sells you a prepaid label in-app or lets you buy your own and upload tracking; and Depop's 10% fee applies to the shipping you charge, not just the item.

Here's the flow in plain English. When you list an item, you choose a shipping option: a Depop prepaid label (weight-based), a custom shipping price you set, or "free shipping." At checkout the buyer pays the item price plus whatever shipping you set. If you used a Depop label, the label cost is deducted from your sale. If you set a custom price or free shipping, you're responsible for buying the label yourself out of your proceeds. (Depop's official help covers the current label options for your region.)

The part almost everyone misses is the third bullet: shipping you charge is taxable by Depop. Which brings us to the first letter.

Letter #1 — "Does Depop take a cut of the shipping I charge?"

"I charge $6 shipping and the item is $30. Does Depop take 10% of the $30 or 10% of the $36? Because that changes what I actually make." — @VINTAGE.MARA, OHIO

Answer: the $36. Depop's 10% selling fee is calculated on the total transaction amount, which includes the shipping price you charge. So on a $30 item + $6 shipping, the fee is 10% of $36 = $3.60, not $3.00.

That extra sixty cents feels trivial until you multiply it by every sale. If you do 40 sales a month averaging $8 shipping, that's ~$32 a month of fee-on-shipping you never budgeted for. This is exactly why I keep telling people to price shipping honestly and to run the whole sale — item, shipping, both fees — through the calculator before listing. (I broke down the full two-bite fee structure in how much Depop takes from a sale.)

"The first time I realized the 10% hit my shipping too, I re-priced my whole shop. That one fact paid for my label printer." — a seller in the desk inbox

Letter #2 — "Who pays for shipping on Depop, me or the buyer?"

"Every platform is different. On Depop, when I sell something, who is actually supposed to pay the postage?" — @FIRSTFLIP.JAY, TEXAS

Answer: usually the buyer — unless you choose free shipping. By default, the buyer pays the shipping cost shown at checkout. If you toggle "free shipping," then you pay the label out of your margin. There is no Depop rule forcing you to pay; it's a pricing decision you make per listing.

The confusion comes from returns, where the rules flip. On a "not as described" return, the seller typically covers return shipping; on a changed-mind return the buyer pays. That's a different conversation — I mapped it platform-by-platform in who pays return shipping, and the Depop-specific version in Depop refund fees. For outbound shipping, though, remember: buyer pays unless you volunteer otherwise.

Letter #3 — "Is free shipping worth it on Depop?"

"Everyone says free shipping makes items sell faster. But I sell $12-$18 tees. If I eat a $4 label, I'm basically working for free. What's the real play?" — @TEESTACK.LEO, FLORIDA

Answer: free shipping is a marketing cost, not a kindness — only buy it when the margin survives. Free shipping can lift visibility and conversion because buyers filter for it and hate surprise costs at checkout. But the label doesn't disappear; it just moves from the buyer's line item to your expenses.

Run Leo's numbers. A $15 tee with a $4.20 label: Depop takes 10% of $15 = $1.50, plus ~$0.60 processing. If the buyer paid shipping, you'd keep ~$15 - $2.10 = $12.90 and the buyer covers the label. If you offer free shipping, you keep $12.90 - $4.20 = $8.70. You just paid $4.20 for the "privilege" of looking attractive in search. On a $15 tee that sourced for $4, your profit collapses. On a $90 jacket with a $6 label, the same trick costs you far less proportionally and may genuinely win the sale.

The fix: use free shipping selectively — on higher-priced items where the label is a small percentage — and on cheap items either charge shipping or bake a couple dollars into the price and call it "free." Never offer it blindly.

Letter #5 — "Can I use my own label instead of Depop's?"

"The in-app label is convenient but I found cheaper rates on a third-party site. Am I allowed to buy my own label and still sell on Depop?" — @LABELHACK.RUI, NEW JERSEY

Answer: yes — buy your own label and just upload the tracking number. Depop doesn't force you to use its label. You can purchase a cheaper label from any carrier or discount service, ship the item, and manually enter the tracking number in the app.

One critical warning, and it's the same one I give in the scams & seller protection guide: always upload the tracking. If you ship on your own label and never enter the tracking, Depop has no proof of delivery — and if the buyer claims "item never arrived," you lose the case and the money. Convenience is nice; protection is non-negotiable.

Letter #6 — "How do I pack clothes so they don't come back 'damaged'?"

"I had a buyer claim a hoodie arrived wet and stained and I lost the dispute. How do people pack so this never happens to them?" — @SECONDHAND.SAM, OREGON

Answer: pack for the worst day the postal system can have, and document everything. Most "damaged in transit" losses are preventable with a boring, repeatable routine. Here's the checklist I wish someone had handed me in year one:

THE PACK-IT-LIKE-A-PRO CHECKLIST

  • Fold neatly and add tissue for higher-priced pieces (perceived value = fewer remorse returns).
  • Wrap in a waterproof poly mailer sized to the item — no giant bag flopping around.
  • For fragile or structured items, add a cardboard stiffener or bubble wrap.
  • Seal securely; reinforce edges with tape on boxes.
  • Include a small thank-you note — it genuinely reduces bad ratings and remorse returns.
  • Photograph the item folded AND inside the mailer before sealing (your dispute evidence).
  • Weigh the final package and buy the correct label weight.

That last photo step is the one that wins disputes. When a buyer claims damage, your pre-seal photo showing a clean, dry, well-packed item is the difference between a refund and a win. (See the Evidence Locker for the full system.)

Letter #7 — "Does Depop ship internationally — and is it worth it?"

"I keep getting messages from buyers in other countries. Should I ship internationally or is that a scam magnet?" — @GLOBALTHRIFT.KIM, WASHINGTON

Answer: Depop supports international sales, but you'll usually use your own label plus a customs form — and you should price for the risk. International orders expand your audience, but they add cost, longer transit, customs paperwork, and higher "never arrived" anxiety. If you do it: use tracked service only, fill the customs form accurately (declare real value), and price shipping honestly rather than eating it.

My honest take: master domestic first. International is a growth lever for established shops with margin to absorb the occasional lost parcel — not a beginner move.

Letter #8 — "What if my item is over 5 lbs or bulky?"

"I sell vintage coats and boots. Some packages hit 6-8 lbs and the label price shocks me. How do heavy sellers survive?" — @HEAVYROT.DEE, MICHIGAN

Answer: for heavy items, abandon prepaid convenience and shop your own label rates. Once you cross ~5 lbs, in-app label pricing often stops being competitive. Compare carrier and discount-service rates, consider slower ground tiers, and charge the buyer accurate shipping instead of offering free. A $120 coat can absorb a $10 label; just make sure the buyer sees it at checkout or your price reflects it.

The Shipping Desk decision flowchart

When you're standing there with the item in hand, use this. It's the whole column compressed into ten seconds:

IF item < 1 lb (tees, light layers) → poly mailer, ground label, charge shipping or bake $3-4 into price.
IF 1–5 lb → compare in-app label vs your own rate; upload tracking either way.
IF > 5 lb / bulky → buy your own label, charge accurate shipping, no free shipping.
IF item > $80 → free shipping is affordable marketing; consider it.
IF item < $20 → never free shipping; the label is your whole margin.
ALWAYS → weigh, photograph before sealing, upload tracking.

The mistakes that cost me (so they don't cost you)

I'll close the desk with my own confessions, because that's the point of this column. I once offered free shipping on a $14 tee "to be competitive" and made $3.10 after fees and the label — less than minimum wage for the packing time. I once guessed a jacket was "about a pound" and it was 1 lb 9 oz, and the label upgrade ate the profit. And I once shipped without entering tracking because I was tired, and when the buyer claimed it never arrived, I had nothing. Every single one of those was avoidable with the checklist above. Shipping isn't glamorous, but it's where small sellers quietly win or lose their month.

Conclusion: price the shipping or eat the shipping — your choice

Depop shipping is simple on the surface and sneaky underneath: the buyer usually pays, Depop taxes the shipping you charge, free shipping is a marketing cost, and the label is a flat tax that punishes cheap items. Weigh it, price it, photograph it, track it. Do those four things and shipping stops being a leak and becomes just another line you control.
