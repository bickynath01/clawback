Depop Shipping Explained: Labels, Costs, Free Shipping & Who Really Pays

Welcome to The Shipping Desk — the column where the exact shipping questions real Depop sellers ask, the ones typed into Google at 1am while a sold item sits unboxed on the kitchen table, get answered the way a friend who's already lost money on shipping would. No corporate fluff, just mechanics, math, and the mistakes worth skipping.

Shipping on Depop looks simple until it isn't. The buyer pays, right? Except Depop takes 10% of that shipping too. And somewhere along the way someone said "free shipping gets you more sales," but nobody mentioned what that does to a $14 tee's margin. So — pull up a chair, here's the whole pile of letters, worked through one at a time.

How this column works: each section is a real question from a seller. The one-sentence answer comes first (grab it and go), then the full explanation, the math, and the fix. Where a question connects to a deeper guide, that link's included — that's where the receipts live.

First, the mechanics: how Depop shipping actually works

The short version: the buyer usually pays shipping at checkout; Depop either sells a prepaid label in-app or lets you buy your own and upload tracking; and Depop's 10% fee applies to whatever shipping gets charged, not just the item price.

Here's the flow in plain English. Listing an item means choosing a shipping option: a Depop prepaid label (weight-based), a custom shipping price, or "free shipping." At checkout the buyer pays the item price plus whatever shipping was set. Using a Depop label means the label cost gets deducted from the sale automatically. Setting a custom price or free shipping means buying the label out of pocket, from the proceeds. (Depop's official help covers current label options by region.)

The part almost everyone misses is that last bit: shipping charged is taxable by Depop. Which brings us straight to the first letter.

Letter #1 — "Does Depop take a cut of the shipping I charge?"

"I charge $6 shipping and the item is $30. Does Depop take 10% of the $30 or 10% of the $36? Because that changes what I actually make." — @VINTAGE.MARA, OHIO

Answer: the $36. Depop's 10% selling fee is calculated on the total transaction amount, which includes the shipping price charged. So on a $30 item + $6 shipping, the fee is 10% of $36 = $3.60, not $3.00.

That extra sixty cents feels trivial until it's multiplied across a whole month. At 40 sales averaging $8 shipping, that's roughly $32 a month of fee-on-shipping that rarely gets budgeted for. This is exactly why running the whole sale — item, shipping, both fees — through the calculator before listing matters more than it seems like it should. (The full two-bite fee structure is broken down in how much Depop takes from a sale.)

Letter #2 — "Who pays for shipping on Depop, me or the buyer?"

"Every platform is different. On Depop, when I sell something, who is actually supposed to pay the postage?" — @FIRSTFLIP.JAY, TEXAS

Answer: usually the buyer — unless free shipping gets chosen. By default, the buyer pays whatever shipping cost is shown at checkout. Toggling "free shipping" flips that — the label comes out of the seller's margin instead. There's no Depop rule forcing either choice; it's a per-listing pricing decision.

The confusion usually comes from returns, where the rules flip again. On a "not as described" return, the seller typically covers return shipping; on a changed-mind return, the buyer pays. That's a different conversation, mapped platform-by-platform in the 2026 Reseller's Matrix, with the Depop-specific version in Depop refund fees. For outbound shipping though: buyer pays unless the seller volunteers otherwise.

Letter #3 — "Is free shipping worth it on Depop?"

"Everyone says free shipping makes items sell faster. But I sell $12-$18 tees. If I eat a $4 label, I'm basically working for free. What's the real play?" — @TEESTACK.LEO, FLORIDA

Answer: free shipping is a marketing cost, not a kindness — only worth it when the margin survives. Free shipping can lift visibility and conversion, since buyers filter for it and dislike surprise checkout costs. But the label doesn't disappear; it just moves from the buyer's line item into the seller's expenses.

Run Leo's numbers through it. A $15 tee with a $4.20 label: Depop takes 10% of $15 = $1.50, plus ~$0.74 processing (2.9% + $0.30). If the buyer pays shipping, the seller keeps roughly $15 − $2.24 = $12.76, and the buyer covers the label separately. Offering free shipping instead means $12.76 − $4.20 = $8.56 kept — paying $4.20 just to look more attractive in search. On a $15 tee sourced for $4, that collapses the profit. On a $90 jacket with a $6 label, the same move costs far less proportionally and may genuinely win the sale.

The fix: use free shipping selectively — on higher-priced items where the label is a small percentage — and on cheap items, either charge shipping outright or bake a couple dollars into the price and call it "free." Never offer it blindly; run the number first, the way the calculator above does.

Letter #5 — "Can I use my own label instead of Depop's?"

"The in-app label is convenient but I found cheaper rates on a third-party site. Am I allowed to buy my own label and still sell on Depop?" — @LABELHACK.RUI, NEW JERSEY

Answer: yes — buy the label independently and just upload the tracking number. Depop doesn't force in-app labels. A cheaper label from any carrier or discount service works fine, ship the item, and enter the tracking number manually in the app.

One critical warning: always upload the tracking. Shipping on an outside label and never entering the tracking number means Depop has no proof of delivery — and if the buyer claims "item never arrived," that dispute is essentially unwinnable without it. Convenience is nice; protection is non-negotiable.

Letter #6 — "How do I pack clothes so they don't come back 'damaged'?"

"I had a buyer claim a hoodie arrived wet and stained and I lost the dispute. How do people pack so this never happens to them?" — @SECONDHAND.SAM, OREGON

Answer: pack for the worst day the postal system can have, and document everything. Most "damaged in transit" losses are preventable with a boring, repeatable routine — here's the checklist worth keeping taped near the packing station:

THE PACK-IT-LIKE-A-PRO CHECKLIST

  • Fold neatly and add tissue for higher-priced pieces (perceived value = fewer remorse returns).
  • Wrap in a waterproof poly mailer sized to the item — no giant bag flopping around.
  • For fragile or structured items, add a cardboard stiffener or bubble wrap.
  • Seal securely; reinforce edges with tape on boxes.
  • Include a small thank-you note — it genuinely reduces bad ratings and remorse returns.
  • Photograph the item folded AND inside the mailer before sealing (dispute evidence).
  • Weigh the final package and buy the correct label weight.

That photo step is the one that actually wins disputes. When a buyer claims damage, a pre-seal photo showing a clean, dry, well-packed item is usually the difference between a refund and a win.

Letter #7 — "Does Depop ship internationally — and is it worth it?"

"I keep getting messages from buyers in other countries. Should I ship internationally or is that a scam magnet?" — @GLOBALTHRIFT.KIM, WASHINGTON

Answer: Depop supports international sales, but a third-party label plus a customs form is usually needed — and the risk should be priced in. International orders expand the buyer pool, but add cost, longer transit, customs paperwork, and higher "never arrived" anxiety. Doing it well means tracked service only, an accurately filled customs form (declaring real value), and pricing shipping honestly rather than absorbing it.

The honest take: master domestic first. International works best as a growth lever for shops with enough margin to absorb the occasional lost parcel — not something to start with.

Letter #8 — "What if my item is over 5 lbs or bulky?"

"I sell vintage coats and boots. Some packages hit 6-8 lbs and the label price shocks me. How do heavy sellers survive?" — @HEAVYROT.DEE, MICHIGAN

Answer: for heavy items, skip prepaid convenience and shop around for actual carrier rates. Past roughly 5 lbs, in-app label pricing often stops being competitive. Comparing carrier and discount-service rates, considering slower ground tiers, and charging accurate shipping instead of offering free tends to be the difference between profit and a wasted sale. A $120 coat can absorb a $10 label; the buyer just needs to see it clearly at checkout, or the price needs to reflect it upfront.

The Shipping Desk decision flowchart

Standing there with the item in hand, this is the whole column compressed into ten seconds:

IF item < 1 lb (tees, light layers) → poly mailer, ground label, charge shipping or bake $3-4 into price.
IF 1–5 lb → compare in-app label vs independent rate; upload tracking either way.
IF > 5 lb / bulky → buy an independent label, charge accurate shipping, no free shipping.
IF item > $80 → free shipping is affordable marketing; worth considering.
IF item < $20 → never free shipping; the label is most of the margin.
ALWAYS → weigh, photograph before sealing, upload tracking.

The mistakes that cost the most (so they don't repeat)

Closing the desk with a few honest observations, since that's really the point of this column. Free shipping on a $14 tee "to be competitive" often results in take-home of $3.10 after fees and the label — well under minimum wage for the packing time involved. Guessing "about a pound" when the package is actually 1 lb 9 oz means the label upgrade quietly eats the entire profit. Shipping without uploading tracking means when a buyer claims it never arrived, there's nothing to point to. Every one of those is avoidable with the checklist above. Shipping isn't glamorous, but it's exactly where small sellers quietly win or lose their month.

Conclusion: price the shipping or eat the shipping — the choice is real

Depop shipping looks simple on the surface and gets sneaky underneath: the buyer usually pays, Depop taxes whatever shipping gets charged, free shipping is a marketing cost dressed up as generosity, and the label is a flat tax that punishes cheap items hardest. Weigh it, price it, photograph it, track it. Do those four things and shipping stops being a leak and becomes just another line that's fully under control.
